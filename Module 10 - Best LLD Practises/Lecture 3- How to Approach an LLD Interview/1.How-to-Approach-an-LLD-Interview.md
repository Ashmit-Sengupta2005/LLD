# How to Approach an LLD Interview

> **The whole thing in one line:** clarify what you're building → find the entities → draw how they interact → turn them into classes → add patterns where they earn their place → handle edge cases.

---

## Part 1 — The 9 steps

| # | Step | What you actually do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1** | **Clarify requirements** | Ask for **functional** (what it does), **non-functional** (scale, latency), and the **hero use case** (the one flow that matters most). Never start designing from assumptions |
| **2** | **Identify core entities** | List the nouns in the requirement, their attributes, and how they relate. Add supporting entities only when needed |
| **3** | **Visualize the flow** | Trace the hero use case step by step: who calls whom, and where external systems (payment, notifications) come in |
| **4** | **Define classes & relationships** | Apply **OOP + SOLID**. Separate **Controller → Service → Repository → Domain**. Program to interfaces, keep coupling loose |
| **5** | **Define use cases & methods** | For each feature: method signature, what it returns, which classes collaborate. Cover CRUD plus anything real-time or background |
| **6** | **Apply design patterns** | Only where a pattern solves a real problem — **and say why**. A pattern without a reason is a red flag |
| **7** | **Handle edge cases** | Concurrency, partial failures, retries and **idempotency**. This is where strong candidates separate themselves |
| **8** | **Class diagram & packages** | Draw the classes and relationships; group packages by layer or by feature |
| **9** | **Future add-ons** | Name one or two extensions and show your design absorbs them **without rewrites** |

> **⭐ The one rule:** spend real time on Step 1. Most failed interviews are solving the wrong problem beautifully.

## Part 2 — Worked example: design **Splitwise**

### First — what does the app actually do?

**Arjun, Meera and Kabir go on a trip.** Nobody pays exactly their share: one pays the hotel, another pays dinner, a third pays cabs. By the end nobody remembers who owes what. **The app tracks it.**

**Day 1 — Arjun pays ₹1500 for dinner, split equally**

Each share = ₹500. Arjun already paid it all, so:

| | Owes Arjun |
| --- | --- |
| Meera | ₹500 |
| Kabir | ₹500 |

That's one **`Expense`** (`amount = 1500`, `paidBy = Arjun`) holding three **`Split`** objects of ₹500 — one per person, recording **that person's share**.

**Day 2 — Meera pays ₹900 for cabs, split equally**

Each share = ₹300, so Arjun and Kabir now owe Meera ₹300 each. The app **nets this against Day 1** instead of storing two separate debts:

```
Meera owed Arjun 500, Arjun now owes Meera 300   →  Meera owes Arjun 200
Kabir owes Arjun 500
Kabir owes Meera 300
```

That running net is the **`BalanceSheet`** — *who owes whom, right now*.

**Day 3 — Kabir pays ₹2000 for the hotel, split by exact amounts**

He took the bigger room: Kabir ₹1000, Arjun ₹500, Meera ₹500. Same flow as before — only **how each share is calculated** changed, from equal to exact.

> **⭐ That's exactly why Strategy is the right pattern here:** creating the expense, building splits and updating balances never changes. Only the calculation does.

**End of trip — settle up**

The app tells Meera *"You owe Arjun ₹200."* She pays by UPI and taps **Settle Up** → a **`Settlement`** is recorded and their balance becomes ₹0.

**Bonus — debt simplification**

```
Meera → Arjun: ₹500
Arjun → Kabir: ₹500          Arjun is just a middleman
```

collapses to **Meera → Kabir: ₹500** — one payment instead of two.

**So the entire app is four operations:**

| Operation | Does |
| --- | --- |
| `addExpense()` | Someone paid → build splits → update balances |
| `getBalances()` | *"You owe Arjun ₹200"* |
| `settleUp()` | Record a payment, clear the debt |
| `simplifyDebts()` | Cut out middlemen, fewer payments |

Now the design.

### Step 1 — Clarify requirements

The interviewer says *"design Splitwise"* — deliberately vague. **This step is the five minutes before you design anything**, and it's graded.

**A. Functional — what it does.** Just list the verbs; they become your methods in Step 5.

- Create users and groups
- Add an expense, recording **who paid**
- Split it **equally / by exact amounts / by percentage**
- Show balances — *"Meera owes Arjun ₹200"*
- Settle up

**B. Non-functional — how well it does it.** Qualities, not features. These are what force your edge cases in Step 7.

| Requirement | What it forces later |
| --- | --- |
| Balances are **always correct** | Locking, atomic updates (Step 7) |
| Two members add expenses **at the same second** | Concurrency handling |
| 5-member trip or a **500-member** group? | Changes the balance data structure |
| Balance view is **instant** | Store running balances; don't recompute from all expenses |

**C. Hero use case — the one flow that matters.**

> **Hero use case** = the single most important and most frequent thing users do with the app — the reason it exists. Also called the *primary* use case.
> *Uber → book a ride. Netflix → play a video. **Splitwise → add an expense.***

> **`addExpense()` → build splits → update balances.**

Pick **one**. It's what you trace in Step 3, the only method you'd write real code for, and what the design is judged on. Settle-up and simplify-debts are secondary.

**D. ⭐ The questions you ask — this is the actual test**

| Ask | Why it changes the design |
| --- | --- |
| *"Group expenses only, or 1-to-1 too?"* | Decides whether `Group` is even mandatory |
| *"Should we simplify debts — A→B→C becomes A→C?"* | An entire extra algorithm, or none |
| *"Multiple currencies?"* | Every amount needs a `Currency` + a conversion service |
| *"Can an expense be edited or deleted after a settlement?"* | Opens a hard consistency problem |

Ask two or three and let them trim the scope. **They'll often say "no, keep it simple"** — and you've just avoided designing something nobody wanted.

> **Why this step decides the interview:** assume multi-currency when they wanted a trip tracker, and you spend 40 minutes on the wrong system. Jumping straight to classes reads as someone who writes code before understanding the ticket.

### Step 2 — Core entities

| Entity | Attributes | Responsibility |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `User` | `id`, `name`, `email` | Identity |
| `Group` | `id`, `name`, `List<User>` | Holds members and expenses |
| `Expense` | `id`, `amount`, `paidBy`, `List<Split>`, `type` | One spend event |
| `Split` | `user`, `amount` | How much **one person** owes for that expense |
| `BalanceSheet` | `Map<User, Map<User, Double>>` | Who owes whom, running total |

#### Core vs supporting entities

**Core** = needed for the hero use case (`addExpense`). **Supporting** = needed for the app to be complete, but not for that main flow. You mention them, then move on.

**`Settlement` — a record of real money actually changing hands**

Every other entity tracks what people *owe*. A settlement records that someone **paid it off**:

```java
class Settlement {
    User from;          // Meera
    User to;            // Arjun
    double amount;      // 200
    Date settledAt;
}
```

Meera UPIs Arjun ₹200 outside the app and taps **Settle Up** → this object is created → their balance drops to ₹0.

> **⭐ Why it isn't just an `Expense`:** an expense says *"money was spent, now split it among people."* A settlement says *"a debt that already existed was repaid."* They move balances in opposite directions and need separate history — mixing them would make "show all trip expenses" include repayments.

**`Notification` — telling members something happened**

When Kabir adds a ₹2000 hotel expense, Arjun and Meera should find out:

```java
class Notification {
    User recipient;
    String message;     // "Kabir added 'Hotel' — your share is ₹500"
    Date createdAt;
}
```

Not needed to compute a single balance, which is exactly why it's supporting — but it's what the **Observer pattern** in Step 6 produces.

> **The interview point:** naming supporting entities shows you see the whole product, while keeping them out of the core shows you know what the hero use case actually needs. Don't design them in detail unless asked.

### Step 3 — Interaction flow (the hero use case)

```
User → ExpenseController.addExpense(request)
         → ExpenseService.validate + build Splits   (SplitStrategy picks how)
             → BalanceSheetService.update(expense)  (who owes whom)
                 → ExpenseRepository.save(expense)
                     → NotificationService.notify(group members)
```

---

### Step 4 — Classes & relationships

```java
interface SplitStrategy {                       // one interface, three behaviours
    List<Split> calculate(double amount, List<User> users, List<Double> values);
}
class EqualSplit      implements SplitStrategy { ... }
class ExactSplit      implements SplitStrategy { ... }
class PercentageSplit implements SplitStrategy { ... }

class ExpenseService {
    private final SplitStrategy strategy;       // injected, not created here
    private final ExpenseRepository repo;
}
```

- `Group` **has many** `User` and `Expense` (composition)
- `Expense` **has many** `Split` (one per participant)
- `ExpenseService` depends on the **`SplitStrategy` interface**, never on `EqualSplit` directly

---

### Step 5 — Core methods

```java
Expense addExpense(ExpenseRequest req);      // the hero flow
Map<User, Double> getBalances(User user);    // "you owe Raj ₹200"
void settleUp(User from, User to, double amount);
```

---

### Step 6 — Patterns, with reasons

| Pattern | Where | **Why** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Strategy** | `SplitStrategy` | Equal / exact / percentage are interchangeable algorithms. Adding "by shares" = a new class, no edits (**OCP**) |
| **Observer** | `NotificationService` | Members must be told when an expense is added — without `ExpenseService` knowing who listens |
| **Factory** | `SplitStrategyFactory` | Turns the request's `type` string into the right strategy object, so no `if/else` leaks into the service |

> **Don't force the rest.** If asked "why no Singleton?" — *"nothing here needs exactly one instance; a shared service is handled by dependency injection."*

---

### Step 7 — Edge cases ⭐

- **Splits don't sum to the total** — reject `[300, 300]` on a ₹500 expense. Validate before saving
- **Floating-point money** — ₹100 split three ways is 33.33 × 3 = 99.99. Store **paise as `long`**, or assign the leftover to one person
- **Concurrent expenses in one group** — two writes to the same balance at once lose an update. Lock the group row, or use **atomic DB updates**
- **Retrying a failed request** — a double-tapped "Add expense" must not create two. Use an **idempotency key**
- **Settling more than owed**, deleting an expense after a settlement, a user leaving with a non-zero balance

---

### Steps 8 & 9 — Structure and extensions

```
controller/   service/   repository/   domain/   strategy/
```

**Future add-ons that cost almost nothing:** a new split type (one class), multi-currency (a `Currency` field + a conversion service), debt simplification (an algorithm over `BalanceSheet`) — **none of them touch `ExpenseService`.** Say that out loud; it proves the design was extensible.

## Part 3 — FAQs

| Question | Answer |
| --- | --- |
| **How much detail?** | High level first, then go deep only where asked |
| **Should I write code?** | Not unless asked. **Class signatures and diagrams** — real bodies only for the hero method |
| **How much business logic?** | Describe it; don't implement it. Say it belongs in the service layer |
| **Which patterns?** | Strategy (interchangeable algorithms), Factory (object creation), Observer (events), Repository (data access), Adapter (third-party APIs) |
| **What's being judged?** | OOP + SOLID, clean class boundaries, **edge cases**, and whether you communicate while you design |

---

## Quick Recall

- **Clarify first** — functional, non-functional, hero use case. Never assume
- **Entities → classes → methods → patterns → edge cases**, in that order
- **Layers:** Controller → Service → Repository → Domain
- **Depend on interfaces**, inject dependencies, keep coupling loose
- **Name a pattern only with a reason** — Strategy for interchangeable algorithms is the safest, most reusable answer
- **Edge cases win interviews:** concurrency, money rounding, retries/idempotency, partial failure
- **Finish by naming an extension** your design handles without modification (**OCP**)
- **Think out loud throughout** — they're grading your reasoning, not just the final diagram
